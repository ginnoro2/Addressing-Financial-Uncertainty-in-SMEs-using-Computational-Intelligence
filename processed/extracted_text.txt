Introduction Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) play a critical role in the economy of Nepal, particularly in urban centers such as Kathmandu, where restaurants and food-related businesses contribute significantly to employment and local commerce. Despite their importance, many SME restaurants operate with informal or semi-formal accounting practices, using financial audit reports mainly for compliance purposes rather than strategic decision-making. With the rise of FinTech platforms, digital lending, and data-driven financial services, there is an increasing demand for automated and intelligent financial analysis tools that can support credit evaluation, risk assessment, and profitability analysis for SMEs. However, most existing financial intelligence and credit scoring models rely on detailed transaction-level data, which is often unavailable or inconsistent in SME environments in developing economies. This research proposes a Financial Intelligence Framework (FIF) that utilizes summarized financial audit data extracted from structured monthly reports to assess the financial risk and profitability of SME restaurants. The data includes consolidated Profit and Loss statements, monthly sales summaries, food margin analysis, product-wise sales reports, purchase and expense records, cash flow statements, creditor and debtor analyses, market sales and wastage reports, and closing stock valuations. The proposed framework is designed and developed using an integrated set of computational intelligence techniques, combining statistical analysis, machine learning, artificial neural networks, time-series forecasting, and explainable AI methods, applied to engineered financial indicators derived from summarized audit tables. Statistical analysis forms the foundational layer of the framework and is employed for descriptive analysis, financial ratio computation, normalization, and trend identification using aggregated fields such as total and net sales, discounts, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, and monthly profitability. These statistical indicators provide baseline insights and serve as input features for subsequent computational intelligence models. Machine learning models including linear and non-linear regression techniques and ensemble-based decision tree algorithms are utilized for financial risk scoring, profitability assessment, and pattern discovery. These models operate on engineered features derived from sales volatility, margin fluctuations, creditor debtor balances, cash flow movements, stock turnover ratios, and wastage indicators. Artificial neural network models, specifically multi-layer perceptron architectures, are further explored for comparative analysis to capture non-linear relationships among aggregated financial variables that may not be fully represented by traditional models. In addition, time-series forecasting techniques are incorporated to predict monthly sales, profit margins, and cash flow trends using historical sales, purchase, and expense data, supporting short- and medium-term financial planning. To enhance transparency and interpretability, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods are applied to interpret model outputs by identifying key financial drivers such as wastage levels, margin pressure, inventory inefficiencies, and liquidity constraints thereby strengthening decision support.

Figure 1: SME restaurants compliance Motivation The motivation for this research arises from a dual gap encompassing both technical and practical dimensions of SME financing in Nepal. From a technical standpoint, the majority of computational finance and credit-risk models are developed under the assumption that rich, structured, and high-frequency data such as transaction logs, payment histories, and digital footprints are readily available. In the context of Nepali SMEs, however, such data is largely absent. Financial information is typically aggregated, irregular, and often limited to audited balance sheets or income statements. This mismatch between model assumptions and real-world data availability renders many existing computational approaches ineffective or inapplicable in the Nepali SME ecosystem. From a practical perspective, the absence of robust data-driven risk assessment mechanisms has led lenders to rely predominantly on collateral requirements or personal guarantees when extending credit to SMEs. While this approach mitigates lender risk, it systematically excludes otherwise viable enterprises that lack sufficient assets or formal guarantees. Consequently, credit access becomes constrained not by business performance or growth potential, but by asset ownership, undermining broader goals of financial inclusion and SME-led economic development. This research seeks to address this practical limitation by exploring computational intelligence techniques capable of supporting credit decisions even in data-constrained environments. In the current lending landscape, human judgment plays a central role in SME credit evaluation, particularly where automated models are unavailable or underdeveloped. However, such judgment is inherently susceptible to cognitive biases that can distort risk assessment. One common bias is recency bias , where lenders place disproportionate weight on recent profitable months while neglecting historical volatility or cyclical downturns. This can lead to overly optimistic credit decisions that fail to account for long-term sustainability.

Another prevalent bias is anchoring , in which decision-makers fixate on a single salient metric such as total sales or revenue while overlooking critical factors like profit margins, cash flow stability, or debt servicing capacity. Additionally, the availability heuristic often leads lenders to equate the presence of a well-maintained ledger or organized financial records with overall financial health, even when underlying issues such as high wastage, excessive leverage, or unpaid liabilities persist. By introducing computational intelligence into SME underwriting, this research aims to reduce reliance on subjective judgment and mitigate these biases through consistent, data-driven inference. Cognitive Biaseness Financial uncertainty assessment is predisposed to systematic cognitive biases, which influence perception, interpretation, and decision-making. In SME restaurant situations, where managerial decisions are frequently experience-driven and resource-constrained, these biases can exacerbate financial instability if not addressed. As a result, the suggested approach includes a methodological bias mitigation layer that is intended to prevent algorithmic systems from reinforcing human judgment errors. Instead of assuming rational actor behavior, this layer explicitly recognizes bounded rationality and incorporates safety into the computational design. Several high-risk cognitive biases are especially pertinent to restaurant financial analysis. Anchoring bias may cause decision-makers to depend too heavily on recent "normal" performance margins, despite structural market shifts. When estimating future performance, the availability heuristic may overestimate the importance of highly memorable events, such as festival-related demand spikes. Survivorship bias can affect benchmarking procedures by focusing on surviving restaurants and disregarding failure precursors. The narrative fallacy can lead to causal interpretations for random fluctuations without statistical validation. Overconfidence bias can lead to excessive reliance on narrow forecast intervals that understate genuine uncertainty ranges. To address these risks, the system includes algorithmic defenses. Bayesian updating algorithms with time-decay weighting are used to mitigate anchoring by gradually diminishing reliance on out-of-date baseline assumptions. Long-memory time-series models are used to lessen availability bias by including broader historical contexts rather than current severe events. Synthetic minority oversampling and the addition of external failure datasets are used to address survivorship bias and ensure that failure patterns inform risk modeling. To avoid narrative fallacy and erroneous correlation detection, causality analysis employs permutation testing and multiple-comparison corrections. Finally, conformal prediction methods are integrated into predictive modeling to ensure statistically correct coverage probabilities while reducing overconfidence in forecast intervals. Implement and Contribute Justification Despite the growing body of literature on computational intelligence and machine learning in financial analytics, existing research remains heavily concentrated on developed economies and transaction-rich datasets, such as credit card logs or bank-level customer histories. There is comparatively limited scholarly work addressing the application of computational intelligence to aggregated SME financial data in South Asia, where firm-level financial information is often incomplete, irregular, and reported in summarized audit formats. In countries like Nepal, SMEs typically produce annual or semi-annual financial statements rather than continuous transactional records, creating a methodological gap that conventional credit-scoring models fail to address. This research responds directly to this gap by focusing on data-constrained environments, thereby contributing novel empirical and methodological insights to the computational intelligence and financial inclusion literature. The rapid expansion of digital financial services in Nepal has led to the emergence of FinTech platforms offering credit products to individuals and small businesses. However, platforms such as eSewa PayLater and FonePay Lending currently rely on simplified or heuristic-based underwriting approaches, primarily due to the absence of robust, SME-specific credit risk models. This limitation restricts their ability to scale SME lending while managing default risk effectively. As SMEs form the backbone of Nepal’s economy, the lack of reliable underwriting frameworks represents a critical industry bottleneck. The proposed research addresses this need by developing computationally intelligent underwriting models tailored to the structural realities of Nepalese SMEs, enabling more accurate risk assessment and supporting sustainable digital lending growth. From a policy perspective, the study aligns closely with Nepal Rastra Bank’s strategic objectives under the Digital Nepal Framework and broader SME financing and financial inclusion initiatives. Regulators have consistently emphasized the importance of data-driven credit expansion to underserved sectors while maintaining financial stability. By proposing models that operate on audited, regulator-acceptable financial summaries, this research supports policy goals without compromising compliance or data privacy. The findings can inform regulators and policymakers on how advanced analytics may be safely integrated into digital lending ecosystems, thereby strengthening evidence-based policymaking in Nepal’s evolving FinTech landscape. Methodologically, the research introduces an innovative application of computational intelligence techniques such as evolutionary algorithms, ensemble learning, or neuro-fuzzy systems to sparse, real-world audit tables rather than high-frequency transaction data. This represents a departure from mainstream FinTech research, which typically assumes access to granular behavioral data. The study demonstrates how meaningful predictive signals can be extracted from low-dimensional, aggregated financial indicators by leveraging feature interaction learning, uncertainty modeling, and adaptive inference mechanisms. As a result, the proposed approach offers a scalable and transferable framework

Diagnostic Analysis Diagnostic analytics takes the paradigm beyond descriptive pattern detection to address the fundamental question of causality specifically to observe the financial changes. Diagnostic analytics uses structured decomposition to convert financial uncertainty into observable causative components, separating controllable operational risks from systemic environmental vulnerabilities. For restaurant SMEs facing financial uncertainty, this analytical layer will distinguish between volatility caused by internal operational decisions and that caused by external market shocks. The hierarchical variance attribution modeling will be used to break down revenue variance into price, volume, and mix impacts to determine structural causes. Granger causality testing will be used to identify time-lag correlations between external indicators and location-specific sales fluctuations. Cost elasticity estimate, which uses regression modeling, will measure how changes in ingredient costs propagate through gross margins, identifying the intensity of exposure to supply shocks. Furthermore, anomaly detection algorithms, such as the Isolation Forest and Local Outlier Factor approaches, will be used to identify anomalous operational trends that require further root-cause analysis. Case Study 1: Tala, Kenya Tala makes microloans to individuals and businesses without traditional credit histories. Instead of bureau scores, it uses alternative digital data, particularly mobile phone and mobile money usage, to evaluate credit risk in data-constrained contexts. Its models convert smartphone and transaction signals into behavioral indicators, including income stability, repayment patterns, and network consistency. Machine learning methods forecast default risk and gradually adjust lending limits. Risk is managed via small initial loans and periodic limit increases. This technique demonstrates how alternative data might increase financial inclusion but also raises questions about privacy and explainability. Case Study 2: Khazna, Egypt Khazna focuses on underbanked workers and micro-merchants, making loan decisions based on summary bookkeeping and cash flow data. Instead of evaluating raw transactions, it examines monthly summaries such as total inflows, expenses, and balance trends. It uses basic rules and ML classifiers to estimate repayment capacity. Because it employs summary financial indications, the system is simpler to audit and understand. Built-in affordability checks and linkages to paychecks or wallets help to mitigate risk. This strategy closely resembles an audit-table approach and is appropriate for markets where digital payments are increasing but bureau data is scarce.
